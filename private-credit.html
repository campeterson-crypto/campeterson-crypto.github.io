How a private credit fund works

Private credit funds take money from big institutional investors and lend it directly to mid-sized companies, most of them owned by private equity firms. Interest comes in, loans get repaid, and the cash flows back through a "waterfall" that decides when investors get their money back and when the manager earns its share of the profit.     Part 3 — Terms worth knowing

    LP (limited partner)
    An investor in the fund: pensions, insurers, endowments, sovereign wealth funds, wealthy families. They commit money up front and the manager draws it down as it finds loans to make.
    GP (general partner)
    The fund manager. It sources, underwrites and monitors the loans, and is paid a management fee plus carried interest.
    PE sponsor
    The private equity firm that owns the borrower. It usually puts in 40–60% of the purchase price as equity, which sits beneath the loan and absorbs losses first.
    Middle market
    Companies too big for a local bank but too small for public bond markets, often with roughly $10–100M of annual EBITDA.
    Senior secured / unitranche
    The loan is first in line to be repaid and is backed by the company's assets. A unitranche rolls what used to be several layers of debt into one loan from one lender.
    SOFR + spread
    Loans are floating-rate: the interest rate is a benchmark (SOFR) plus a fixed spread, so income rises when rates rise.
    OID / upfront fee
    The lender funds slightly less than face value (say 98 cents per dollar) but is repaid in full, an extra 1–3% of return.
    Fund leverage
    Many direct lending funds borrow from banks against their loan portfolio, often around 1x, to amplify returns on investors' equity.
    Preferred return (hurdle)
    The annual return LPs must earn, typically 5–8% in private credit, before the manager earns any carry.
    Catch-up
    Once LPs have their capital and pref, the GP gets most or all of the next dollars until it has "caught up" to its carry share of total profit.
    Carried interest (carry)
    The GP's share of profits, often 10–15% in private credit (lower than the ~20% typical in private equity), paid only after LPs get their money back plus the pref.
    MOIC and IRR
    MOIC is total money returned divided by money invested. IRR is the annualized return that accounts for when the cash came back.

    An educational, simplified illustration. It doesn't describe any specific fund, and real funds differ in fee bases, reinvestment, timing of capital calls, waterfall terms and taxes.
